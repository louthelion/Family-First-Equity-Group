LOCAL · The Housing Market

Orlando housing market close to balanced as sellers close at about 96% of asking

November 11, 2026

Greater Orlando is sitting close to balanced — the middle ground between a buyer’s market and a seller’s market. The measure that shows it is months of supply: 4.8 months. In plain words, that is how long the homes currently for sale would take to sell at the recent pace of buying if no new homes came onto the market. At 4.8 months, neither side holds a clear advantage: sellers cannot count on a rush of competing buyers, and buyers cannot count on desperate sellers.

The price numbers tell the same steady story. Over the 12 months through September 2026, the median sold price in Greater Orlando was $397,000 — the middle of what homes actually sold for, not what they were first listed at. The typical seller closed at about 96% of the original asking price, which means the first list price is best understood as a starting point for negotiation, not the final number. And homes are still changing hands in real numbers: 2,812 homes closed across the four counties in October, so this is a market that is moving, not one that has frozen.

What this means in plain words for a Central Florida homeowner: the market will reward a fair price and punish a hopeful one. When supply sits near balance, an asking price set from what similar nearby homes actually sold for gives a home its best chance to move, while a price set well above the sold evidence tends to sit — and a home that sits invites lower offers later. Condition and presentation matter more in a balanced market too, because buyers have other homes to choose from and compare against yours on the same weekend.

For an investor, a balanced market is a numbers market. There is no rising tide doing the work for you, so a purchase has to make sense on the day it closes: the price paid, the work the property honestly needs, and the rent or resale the real numbers support. The opportunity in this kind of market is patience — sellers closing at about 96% of asking are showing, in the open, that a well-supported offer below asking can still get a deal done.

Source: Stellar MLS data reported by The Homes in Orlando Team — November 2026

WORLDWIDE · Worldwide Housing

Hong Kong luxury homes surge while much of the world cools

November 10, 2026

Hong Kong’s luxury housing market is running hot while much of the world’s housing cools. Luxury home prices there are up 5.3% so far this year through September 2026. That is not a one-month blip: prices now stand 11.3% above their March 2025 low, which means the top of that market has been climbing back, step by step, for well over a year — a recovery at the very top end even while ordinary housing in many countries stays quiet.

The sales count shows how strong the top end is. Between January and September, 197 homes sold for more than HK$100 million each — up 109.6% from a year earlier, more than double. These are trophy properties changing hands at a pace the market has not seen in the comparison period, and each one is a large amount of money moving into a small number of homes. When the most expensive homes in a city sell faster and for more, it pulls the whole luxury tier’s confidence up with it.

Who is buying explains a lot of it. Mainland Chinese buyers accounted for 72% of luxury primary-market deals in the third quarter — nearly three out of every four. In plain words, this surge is powered mainly by money arriving from outside the city, aimed at a supply of top-end homes that is scarce by nature. When outside money chases a small number of properties, prices can rise quickly even if the wider economy and the everyday housing market feel flat.

The lesson travels to Central Florida, and it cuts both ways. A headline about luxury prices jumping in one famous city is not a reading of what a house in Orlando, Kissimmee, or Sanford is worth — the top of one market moves on its own forces, including money arriving from abroad. What does apply here is the habit: watch where outside money is flowing, but judge your own home or your own purchase by local sold prices, local supply, and local days on market. That is the yardstick that decides real deals in Central Florida.

Source: CBRE data — reported November 10, 2026

LOCAL · Interest Rates

Orlando home values ease while homes still go under contract in about six weeks

November 8, 2026

Home values in Greater Orlando have eased a little without the market standing still. The typical home value was $383,302, down 1.4% from a year ago. In plain words, that is a gentle softening, not a slide: the typical home is worth slightly less than it was a year ago, after a long run of gains. This reading is Zillow’s typical-value measure, which is a different yardstick from the sold-price median in the Stellar MLS story above, so the two numbers will not match exactly — each story on this page credits its own source and its own measure.

Homes are still moving at a workable pace, and buyers have real choice. A home was taking about 41 days to go under contract — roughly six weeks from listing to an accepted offer. That is slower than the rush years, when good homes went in days, and it gives a buyer time to look, compare, and think. Choice is the other half of the picture: 16,482 homes were for sale, so a buyer who walks away from one home has many others to consider, and a seller is competing against a long list every single day.

Financing is still the heavy part of the monthly payment. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate stood at 7.18% in November 2026. In plain words, at a rate like that, interest takes a large share of every monthly payment, and the same income supports a smaller loan than it did when rates were low. That is why price and rate have to be read together: a slightly lower price does not automatically mean a comfortable payment, and a buyer’s real budget is set by the payment, not by the headline price alone.

For a Central Florida seller, the plain reading is that a fairly priced home still moves — about six weeks to contract is a normal, healthy pace — but pricing has to respect the buyer’s payment math and the 16,000-plus other homes on offer. For a buyer or an investor, this is a market that rewards homework: time on the market is room to negotiate on price or terms, and any purchase should be run at today’s 7.18% rate, not at a lower rate someone hopes arrives later. A deal that only works if rates fall is a hope, not a deal.

Source: Zillow Research and Freddie Mac data via FRED — November 2026

UNITED STATES · Real Estate Economy

Big landlords are now selling more Central Florida homes than they buy

November 4, 2026

The largest landlords have flipped from buyers to net sellers in Central Florida. Invitation Homes, the biggest name in that group, sold 658 homes while buying 216 in the third quarter of 2026 — roughly three homes sold for every one bought. For years these companies were the competition a local family faced at every starter-home listing; that has reversed. When the biggest buyer in the room becomes a seller, the whole feel of the market for ordinary homes changes with it.

The listing numbers show the scale of the turn. Institutional listings more than doubled over the year, rising from 4,166 in February to 9,447 in July, according to Parcl Labs. And these homes are being priced to move, not to test the market: 54% of institutional listings carry a price cut, compared with 38.7% of all listings. In plain words, more than half of the homes the big landlords are selling have already had their price reduced at least once — a sign of sellers who would rather close than wait.

Federal law is part of the shift. The ROAD to Housing Act, in effect since July 11, 2026, bars investors who already own 350 or more single-family homes from buying more. In plain words, the very largest owners can no longer keep adding houses to their holdings, which removes their buying power from the market at the same moment many of them are listing what they already hold. Policy, not just price, is pushing these homes back toward the open market and toward individual buyers.

For Central Florida families, the hopeful reading is real: more starter homes coming back to the market means more chances to buy a home that, a few years ago, might have gone to a landlord’s portfolio instead. For an owner thinking of selling, the other side of the same story matters — your home may now compete with institutional listings on nearby streets, many of them already price-cut. That makes your own pricing, condition, and timing worth getting right from day one, ideally against the sold prices on your own street, not against the highest asking price you can find.

Source: Parcl Labs data and Invitation Homes results, reported by Homes in Orlando — November 4, 2026

Books we recommend — the real-estate learning shelf

These are real books by named authors — the shelf we point a new investor to first. Each one is summarised below in our own words, with a free-value note: what you can learn for free from the summary alone, before you buy anything or borrow it from your library.

Cover slot

The Millionaire Real Estate Investor

By Gary Keller, with Dave Jenks and Jay Papasan

Summary: how top investors think about money, models, and building a portfolio — the mindset and the math behind buying right. Keller draws on interviews with investors who built serious holdings and lays out the patterns they share: they think in terms of clear financial criteria before they look at a house, they use proven models for finding, evaluating, and buying, and they treat each purchase as one step in a portfolio, not a gamble on a single property.

Free value from this summary: you learn, for free, that buying right starts before the property — set your numbers and your buying criteria first, judge every deal against those models instead of hope or pressure, and ask what a purchase does for the whole portfolio, not just whether the house looks good.

The Book on Rental Property Investing

By Brandon Turner

Summary: a practical playbook for buying and holding rentals — finding deals, running the numbers, financing, and managing for long-term cash flow. Turner walks the full sequence an investor actually follows: decide on a plan, hunt for properties that can pay, work the figures honestly before offering, line up financing, and then manage tenants and costs so the rent keeps coming in month after month.

Free value from this summary: you learn, for free, the order of work that keeps a rental honest — plan first, find second, run the numbers before you offer, finance on terms the rent can carry, and manage as part of the return, not as an afterthought. A deal that only works if everything goes perfectly is not a deal.

The ABCs of Real Estate Investing

By Ken McElroy

Summary: finding properties that pay, evaluating them honestly, and the team and systems behind profitable investing. McElroy explains how to spot a property with real income potential, how to work through its actual numbers and condition instead of its asking story, and why no investor does this alone — the right team, good management, and repeatable systems are what turn one good purchase into a business that keeps paying.

Free value from this summary: you learn, for free, a plain test for any property — does it pay, on its real numbers today, not on a promised future price — and that honest evaluation plus a capable team matters more than hunting for a secret bargain. If the income is not there on paper, it will not appear at closing.
