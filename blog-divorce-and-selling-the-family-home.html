Divorce and Selling the Family Home

Divorce and selling the family home in plain terms: the written agreement, who may sign, how the house is cared for while decisions are made, and questions for a Florida attorney.

Blog Post summary

Divorce and selling the family home in plain terms: the written agreement, who may sign, how the house is cared for while decisions are made, and questions for a Florida attorney.

A house decision inside a larger legal process

When a marriage ends, the family home is usually one of the largest things the couple owns together, and what happens to it is decided inside the divorce process rather than apart from it. The house might be sold, one person might keep it, or a decision might be postponed under terms the court approves. Which path applies depends on the couple's documents, their agreement, and Florida law. This post is general information only and is not legal advice. It describes the questions families commonly work through, in plain terms, so a conversation with a Florida attorney can start from an organized file instead of from memory.

Start with what is written down

Before anyone talks with a buyer, gather the papers that describe the house and the marriage's claim on it: the deed, any loan statements, tax and insurance records, and any court orders or written agreements already in place about the property. Read the deed for whose names appear on it and how ownership is stated, because the names on the deed and the terms of the divorce both shape who must join in a sale. If a court order or a signed agreement already says what happens to the house, that document leads. If nothing has been decided yet, say so plainly rather than assuming an outcome, and let the legal process settle the question before a sale is promised to anyone.

Who may sign, and why title matters

A sale needs signatures from everyone the deed and the divorce require, and a title company will check that authority before a closing can happen. That is why a sale arranged by one person alone, without the other owner's agreement or a court order behind it, can stall at the closing table or unravel afterward. Where both owners agree to sell, the written divorce agreement or court order usually states how the sale is handled and how what remains after loans and closing costs are settled is divided. Those terms belong in writing, reviewed by the professionals handling the divorce, before a buyer is chosen — not reconstructed from conversation after an offer arrives.

Caring for the house while decisions are made

Divorce proceedings take the time they take, and the house still needs care in the meantime. Loan payments, insurance, taxes, utilities, and basic upkeep continue while the case moves, and letting any of them lapse can harm both owners and complicate a later sale. Agree in writing, even informally at first, on who is living in the home, who is paying which bills, and how access for any review or showing will be arranged. Keep a simple dated record of payments and repairs, and keep communication about the property factual and in writing. A house that arrives at its sale decision insured, maintained, and documented is far easier to sell calmly than one that has been neglected while the family argued about it.

Questions to bring to a Florida attorney

An attorney licensed in Florida can answer the questions general reading cannot: what the court expects for this family's situation, whether a sale may proceed now or must wait, whose signatures a deed will require, and how the proceeds are to be handled under the agreement or order. Bring the deed, the loan statements, and any existing orders or agreements to that conversation, and ask what else the file needs. A real estate attorney or the title company can separately confirm what the closing itself will require.
