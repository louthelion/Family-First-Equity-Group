Selling a House with Code Violations

A plain-language guide to selling a house with open code violations: read the notice carefully, confirm what is actually open, and keep an honest paper trail.

Blog Post summary

A plain-language guide to selling a house with open code violations: read the notice carefully, confirm what is actually open, and keep an honest paper trail.

What a code violation notice means

A code violation notice is a written notice from a city or county saying that something about a property does not meet a local rule. It might concern overgrown vegetation, an unpermitted structure, a damaged fence, an unsafe condition, or work done without a required permit. A notice is not a verdict on the whole house, and it does not by itself make a property unsellable. It is a specific claim about a specific condition, and the first job is to understand exactly what it says.

Find out what is actually open

Notices can be old, partially resolved, or already cleared without the owner realizing it. Ask the issuing office for the current status of each item, and request the records in writing: the original notice, any inspection notes, and any clearance or closure letters. Separate open items from closed ones, and separate items that concern the structure from items that concern upkeep of the lot. A buyer, a title professional, and an attorney all need the same clear list, and the owner is the person best placed to assemble it.

Violations and honest disclosure

Known code issues should be written down plainly, just like any other known condition of the property. Hiding a violation does not remove it: the issuing office keeps its own records, and a buyer or title search may surface the item later, when it is harder and more stressful to address. Sellers can have duties to share known information, and the exact duties depend on the property and the transaction. Ask a qualified attorney or title professional what must be disclosed in your situation.

Fix, credit, or sell as-is — the choices

An open violation usually leaves the owner with a small set of paths, and each one deserves a calm comparison. Fixing the item may mean hiring licensed help, pulling a permit, and waiting for a re-inspection. Leaving it open may mean a buyer asks for a price adjustment or a credit at closing, or accepts the property as-is and takes the item on. Some violations are simple upkeep; others involve permits, contractors, or hearings. Get written estimates and written guidance before choosing, and do not start work that requires a permit until you know what the permit requires.

Keep the paper trail together

From the first notice to the final clearance letter, keep every document in one file: notices, inspection reports, permit applications, contractor estimates and receipts, correspondence with the issuing office, and any hearing paperwork. Note the date on each document and keep a short log of every call, including who you spoke with and what was said. If a violation is cleared, get the clearance in writing and keep it with the deed file. A complete paper trail turns a worrying notice into a documented item with a status, an owner, and a next step — which is exactly what buyers and professionals need to see.
