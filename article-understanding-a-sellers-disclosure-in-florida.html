Understanding a Seller's Disclosure in Florida

Understanding a seller's disclosure in Florida, in plain terms: what the disclosure is for, what kinds of things it covers, how to prepare one honestly, and questions for a Florida attorney.

Article summary

This guide emphasizes answering from records rather than memory, describing known conditions plainly, and questions for a Florida attorney or the title company before a disclosure is signed. What a seller's disclosure is

A seller's disclosure is a written statement in which the seller of a home tells the buyer what the seller actually knows about the property's condition and history. Its purpose is honesty in writing: the buyer receives, before closing, a record of known problems, past repairs, and conditions that could matter to someone about to own the house, and the seller gains a clear record of having shared them. In Florida, sellers of residential property are expected to disclose known facts that materially affect the value of the property and are not readily observable by the buyer. This article explains, in plain terms, what that expectation involves and how a seller prepares for it. It is general information only and is not legal advice, and it does not replace the guidance of a Florida attorney for a specific property.

What the disclosure usually covers

The questions on a disclosure form reach across the whole property. They commonly ask about the roof and its history, water intrusion and flooding, plumbing and electrical systems, heating and cooling, the condition of appliances and fixtures, additions or work done with or without permits, and any known issues with boundaries, shared features, or neighborhood matters the seller is aware of. They may ask about environmental concerns, past insurance claims, termite or pest history, and whether the property sits in an area with known flood risk. A seller is answering about what they know, not guaranteeing what they have never seen — which is why the honest answers include the option of saying that something is unknown, where the form allows it, rather than guessing.

Known, unknown, and not readily observable

Three ideas do most of the work in a disclosure. The first is knowledge: the duty concerns facts the seller actually knows, learned from living in the house, from records, or from professionals who inspected or repaired it. The second is materiality: the facts that matter are the ones that would affect the value of the property or a buyer's decision, not every scuff and quirk of an ordinary lived-in home. The third is observability: a condition a buyer can plainly see on a walk-through is different from one hidden inside a wall or buried in the property's past, and it is the hidden, known facts that the disclosure exists to surface. Holding those three ideas apart keeps the task in proportion. A seller is not being asked to certify that a house is perfect, and a buyer is not being promised a new house; both are being given the same written picture of what the seller knows.

Preparing the disclosure from records, not memory

The most reliable disclosure is assembled from paper. Before completing the form, gather the records that carry the property's history: past inspection reports, repair invoices, roof and system service records, insurance claim correspondence, permit records for work that was done, and any notices from the county, the city, or a homeowners association. Walk the property slowly with the form in hand and compare each question against what the rooms actually show. Where memory and records disagree, records win, and where neither answers a question, writing that the matter is unknown is more honest than a confident guess. Sellers who no longer live in the property — including families selling an inherited house — should say plainly what their knowledge is based on, because a disclosure written from a distance and labeled as such serves a buyer better than one that pretends to firsthand knowledge it does not have.

How the disclosure moves through a sale

The disclosure is usually prepared early, because buyers rely on it while deciding, and because surprises discovered later can unravel an agreement that was otherwise sound. The seller completes and signs it, the buyer receives and reviews it, and it becomes part of the sale file alongside the agreement and the title work. If a seller learns something new about the property after the disclosure is delivered — a leak that appears, a notice that arrives — the careful course is to share the new fact in writing as well, through the same channel, and keep a copy. Where a buyer arranges their own inspection, the inspection and the disclosure answer different questions: the inspection reports what a professional can observe, while the disclosure reports what the seller knows. Both belong in the file, and neither replaces the other.

Honesty is the protection, for both sides

Sellers sometimes worry that disclosing a problem will cost them the sale, and so feel tempted to soften or omit what they know. Experience runs the other way. A known problem disclosed in writing becomes a fact the parties can price, repair, or accept together, inside the agreement. The same problem discovered after closing becomes a dispute about what was hidden, with the signed disclosure as the central document. Buyers, for their part, generally trust a seller whose disclosure is specific and candid, including about repairs that were made and problems that were fixed, because a house with an honest history is easier to buy than a house with a silent one. Where a sale is made as-is, the disclosure still matters: as-is describes the condition in which the property is being sold, not a permission to withhold known facts. Family First Equity Group buys property as a real estate investor and reads disclosures as part of its review; a complete, candid disclosure speeds that review rather than slowing it, and requesting a review never obligates a seller to accept anything.

Questions a seller should resolve before signing

A disclosure is a signed statement, and it deserves the same care as any other signed document in a sale. Before signing, a seller should be able to say how each answer was arrived at, which records support it, and where an answer reflects limited knowledge rather than certainty. Questions about what Florida law requires for a particular property — including properties held by an estate, properties with unusual histories, and situations where the seller never lived in the home — belong with a Florida attorney, and the time to ask them is before the form is signed, not after a buyer questions an answer. The title company or closing attorney handling the sale can also confirm how the completed disclosure is delivered and kept in the file. A seller who prepares the disclosure from records, answers plainly, and asks those questions early has done the job the form exists to do.

Documents to have ready

  • The deed and any documents showing ownership and authority to sell.
  • Past inspection reports for the property, if any exist.
  • Repair invoices and service records for the roof and major systems.
  • Insurance claim correspondence relating to the property.
  • Permit records for additions or work done on the property.
  • Any notices from the county, the city, or a homeowners association.
  • Records of water intrusion, flooding, or drainage problems, if any.
  • A written list of known problems and of repairs already completed.
  • Notes on anything the seller does not know, stated plainly as unknown.
  • A cover note listing what is present, missing, or still being requested.

Common mistakes

  • Completing the disclosure from memory instead of from records.
  • Guessing at an answer rather than stating that a matter is unknown.
  • Omitting a known problem because it was repaired, without saying so.
  • Assuming an as-is sale removes the need to disclose known facts.
  • Waiting until late in the sale to prepare the disclosure.
  • Failing to share, in writing, a new problem learned after delivery.
  • Signing the disclosure before questions about it have been answered.

Questions to ask before the next step

  • What does the seller actually know about this property, and from what records?
  • Which answers rest on firsthand knowledge, and which on limited knowledge?
  • Are there known facts that affect value and are not readily observable?
  • What records support each answer about repairs, claims, and permits?
  • Has anything about the property changed since the disclosure was delivered?
  • How and when will the completed disclosure be delivered to the buyer?
  • Which questions about this property belong with a Florida attorney before signing?
