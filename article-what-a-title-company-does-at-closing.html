What a Title Company Does at Closing

What a title company does at closing, from the title search and commitment to escrow, signing, recording, and title insurance, explained in plain terms.

Article summary

What a title company does at closing, from the title search and commitment to escrow, signing, recording, and title insurance, explained in plain terms. This guide emphasizes verified records, independent review, and questions for qualified title and legal professionals before any commitment.

The title company's job in plain terms

A title company is the neutral party that checks whether a seller can legally transfer a property, holds the money and documents while the transfer is completed, and records the result with the county. It does not represent the buyer or the seller the way an attorney or an agent would. Its job is the record itself: confirm ownership, find the claims against the property, make sure those claims are dealt with, and leave a clean, recorded trail behind the sale.

That neutrality is the point. Both sides rely on the same verified facts, the same written figures, and the same recorded documents. When a title company does its work well, the closing feels uneventful — and uneventful is exactly what a property transfer should feel like.

Before closing: the title search

Long before anyone sits at a closing table, the title company orders a title search: a structured review of the public records for the specific property. The searcher traces the chain of deeds to confirm who owns the property, and looks for recorded mortgages, liens, judgments, tax matters, easements, and other items that affect ownership. Old loans that were paid off but never formally released, second mortgages nobody mentioned, and ownership questions involving estates, trusts, or multiple owners are the kinds of items a search is designed to find.

The search is the reason to involve a title company early rather than late. An issue found early is a homework item with time to resolve. The same issue found in the final week before closing is a crisis. Owners who suspect loans, liens, old paperwork, or ownership questions can ask for a records review before a buyer exists.

The title commitment: a homework list, not a verdict

The search results are usually summarized in a title commitment. The commitment states the current ownership as the records show it, lists the items that must be resolved before closing, and lists the matters a title policy would not cover. Read the requirements section as a homework list: payoff figures from lenders, releases of old claims, documents proving an owner's authority, or corrections to the record. Read the exceptions section as information, not as a judgment on the property.

Do not interpret a commitment alone, and do not ignore lines you do not understand. Ask the title professional to walk through it item by item: what each entry is, where it came from, what resolves it, and who is responsible for resolving it. Write down the answers with dates. A commitment reviewed this way becomes a schedule of work instead of a source of anxiety.

Clearing the items on the list

Most of the title company's pre-closing work is coordination: requesting written payoff figures from lenders, confirming that old claims have been released or arranging for their release, collecting authority documents from estates, trusts, or entities, and correcting errors in the record where correction is possible. The figure that matters for a loan payoff is the lender's written payoff amount calculated to a specific date — not the balance printed on a monthly statement, because interest and possible fees continue to be calculated until the payoff date.

Some items cannot be cleared by paperwork alone and need an attorney, a lender conversation, or a court process. The title company will say so, and that honesty is valuable: it tells the owner which professional to bring in, instead of letting the issue surface at the closing table.

Escrow: holding money and documents safely

At closing, the title company usually acts as the escrow holder. Escrow means a neutral party holds the buyer's funds and the signed documents until every condition of the transfer is met, and only then releases them according to written instructions. The seller's recorded loans are normally paid from the sale proceeds through this process, and documents showing the debts as satisfied are recorded afterward.

Escrow protects both sides at once. The buyer does not hand over funds before the deed and the title work are ready, and the seller does not hand over the deed before the funds are in place. The written settlement statement — the line-by-line accounting of the transaction — is the document to read carefully before signing anything, and to ask questions about while there is still time to correct it.

The closing table: who signs what

At the closing itself, the title company (or a closing agent working with it) presents the documents, explains each one in general terms, collects signatures, and notarizes what must be notarized. The seller generally signs the deed transferring ownership and documents related to payoff and disclosure. The buyer generally signs the loan documents, if there is a loan, and the documents accepting the property and its title condition.

Every owner generally has to join in the sale, and ownership is not always a single name on a deed. If a property is held by spouses, siblings, a trust, an estate, or a business entity, each arrangement raises its own question about who must sign and what documents prove their authority. A missing, unreachable, or disagreeing owner is an issue to resolve before closing, not during it.

Recording: making the transfer official

After signing, the title company sends the deed and related documents to the county recording office, where they become part of the public record. Recording is what makes the transfer visible to the world: future searches will show the new owner, and future buyers, lenders, and title companies will rely on that record. The buyer receives the recorded deed and, if one was issued, the title insurance policy. The seller should keep copies of the settlement statement, the recorded deed, and every payoff and release document in the property file.

Recording can take time after the closing appointment, and that gap is normal. What matters is that the documents were delivered for recording and that the owner can confirm, with the title company, that they were recorded correctly.

Title insurance, in plain terms

Title insurance is a policy that protects against certain losses from title defects that were not found before closing — for example, a recording error, a forged document in the chain of deeds, or an undisclosed heir. A lender's policy protects the lender; an owner's policy protects the buyer. They are separate policies, and the owner's policy is the one that protects the person who now owns the home.

Title insurance is different from other insurance because it looks backward: it covers problems rooted in the past, before the policy was issued, rather than events that happen afterward. Like the commitment, the policy has exclusions, and the time to ask what is and is not covered is before closing, with the title professional explaining the policy in plain terms.

What a title company does not do

A title company is not your attorney, your lender, or your agent. It does not give legal advice about whether you should sell, negotiate your price, explain the tax consequences of your sale, or decide which path is best when debts may approach what a sale could bring. Those questions belong with an attorney, a tax professional, and the other advisors you choose. The title company's role is narrower and just as important: verified records, neutral handling of funds and documents, and a clean recorded result.

Understanding that boundary helps owners use the right professional for each question instead of expecting one office to answer all of them — and helps owners notice when a question has been left with the wrong desk.

How to work well with your title company

Start early, bring an organized file, and ask questions in writing. Gather statements for every mortgage and home equity line, property tax notices and payment receipts, association statements if the property has an association, the owner's title policy from the original purchase if one was issued, and documents showing every owner's name and authority to act. Add a dated list of work performed on the property, with contractor names, and the contact information of every owner and every lender. Label estimates and unknowns honestly.

Then stay in contact: confirm the search is ordered, review the commitment item by item, ask what remains open and who owns each item, and review the settlement statement before the closing appointment rather than at it. A seller who hands over an organized file with its gaps labeled will move through closing faster and with fewer misunderstandings than one who hands over a shoebox — and a title company that answers questions clearly is one worth keeping for the next transaction.

Documents to have ready

  • The deed and any prior title policy from the original purchase.
  • Statements for every mortgage and home equity line.
  • Property tax notices and payment receipts.
  • Association statements, if the property has an association.
  • Documents showing every owner's name and authority to act.
  • A dated list of work performed on the property, with contractor names.
  • Contact information for every owner and every lender.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a paid-off loan was formally released in the records.
  • Using a monthly statement balance as the payoff amount.
  • Leaving a second loan or home equity line off the list.
  • Assuming one owner can sign on behalf of everyone.
  • Waiting until the closing appointment to read the settlement statement.
  • Treating the title company as a substitute for an attorney.

Questions to ask before the next step

  • What claims are recorded against this property today?
  • Which items must be resolved before title can transfer?
  • Who must sign, and what documents prove their authority?
  • What are the exact written payoff figures, and through what dates are they valid?
  • What does the settlement statement show, line by line?
  • Has the deed been recorded, and can we confirm it?
  • What does the owner's title policy cover, and what does it exclude?
  • Which questions belong with an attorney rather than the title company?
