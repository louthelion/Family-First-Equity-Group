Documents to Gather Before Selling

Documents to gather before selling a house, from ownership and loan papers to tax, condition, and occupancy records, organized so professionals can review the file without delay.

Article summary

Documents to gather before selling a house, from ownership and loan papers to tax, condition, and occupancy records, organized so professionals can review the file without delay. This guide emphasizes verified records, labeled gaps, and questions for qualified title, legal, and tax professionals before any commitment.

Why the file matters before a buyer appears

Most delays in a property sale are not caused by the house itself. They are caused by a missing paper, an unclear owner, an old loan that was never formally released, or a question nobody asked until closing week. Gathering the file before a buyer appears turns those surprises into homework items with time to resolve. A seller who hands over an organized file — with its gaps labeled honestly — gives a title company, an attorney, and a buyer the same clear picture, and that shared picture is what keeps a sale moving calmly.

The goal is not perfection. Few sellers have every document at hand on the first day. The goal is a single, honest file: what you have, what is missing, where you have asked for it, and what you do not yet know. That file is worth assembling even if a sale is still only a possibility.

Ownership and authority documents

Start with proof of who owns the property and who may sell it. The deed is the central document: it names the owners and describes the property as the public record shows it. Add any will or trust documents, probate or estate papers, and entity documents if the property is held by a business, along with the owner's title policy from the original purchase if one was issued. Where more than one person holds an interest, gather the contact information and authority documents for every owner, not just the person leading the sale.

Authority questions deserve attention early. Spouses, siblings, heirs, trustees, and representatives of an estate each raise their own question about who must sign and what proves they may. A missing, unreachable, or disagreeing owner is an issue to resolve before a buyer exists, not at the closing table. An attorney can explain what the family's documents require; the seller's job is to bring those documents together so the question can be answered.

Loan and lien paperwork

Every loan secured by the property belongs in the file, including mortgages, home equity lines, and any private or family loans recorded against it. Gather the most recent statement for each one, the lender's contact information, and any letters about payoff, default, forbearance, or modification. Do not rely on a monthly statement balance as the payoff figure: the amount a lender requires to release a loan is a written payoff figure calculated to a specific date, because interest and possible fees continue until that date.

Add any notices of liens, judgments, or claims that may touch the property, including tax matters and association claims, and any releases showing that an old claim has been satisfied. An old loan that was paid off but never formally released in the public record is one of the most common title surprises, and it is far easier to clear with the lender's records in hand than from memory alone.

Tax, insurance, and association records

Property tax bills and payment receipts show whether taxes are current and what remains due, and they help a closing professional prepare an accurate settlement statement. Homeowners insurance policies and recent premium notices show the coverage in place, and association statements show dues, special assessments, and any outstanding balances if the property belongs to an association. Gather the most recent version of each, and note where a bill is paid through an escrow account rather than directly, so no one double-counts or misses a payment.

Keep these records with the loan papers rather than in a separate drawer. A closing review reads them together: taxes, insurance, association dues, and loan payoffs all draw from the same sale proceeds, and a complete set lets the settlement statement be checked line by line before anyone signs.

Condition and work history

A dated list of work performed on the property — with contractor names, permit information, receipts, and warranties — answers questions buyers and inspectors will ask anyway. Include permits and final inspection approvals for additions, conversions, roof or system replacements, and any work that required a permit, along with warranties for work still covered. Add photographs of the property's condition, dated if possible, and any inspection reports, repair estimates, or contractor assessments the owner already has.

Be honest about what is unknown. An undocumented repair, an unpermitted addition, or a condition the owner has not investigated should be labeled as unknown rather than described from guesswork. Buyers, inspectors, and title professionals can work with a labeled gap; they cannot work with a confident statement that turns out to be wrong.

Occupancy, tenancy, and service papers

If anyone lives in the property besides the owner, the file needs the lease or occupancy agreement, the tenant's contact information, the rent and deposit terms as written, and any notices already given or received. If the property is vacant, note that plainly, along with who holds keys and who checks on the home. Add service contracts that run with the property — lawn care, pest control, security, pool service, or a home warranty — and note which contracts transfer, which end at sale, and which require notice to cancel.

Tenancy and service details affect timing, access for showings and inspections, and what a buyer inherits at closing. A seller who discloses them early avoids a late discovery that could delay a closing or sour a negotiation that was otherwise ready.

What to do when a document is missing

A missing document is a request, not a dead end. A recorded deed can usually be obtained from the county recording office. A lender can provide statements and a written payoff figure. A tax collector can confirm tax status and payment history. An association can provide statements and resale documents. An attorney or title company can request a records review and explain what else the public record requires. For each missing item, write down what was requested, from whom, and when, and keep the reply with the file.

Some gaps cannot be filled by a request alone — an unreleased loan, a disputed ownership question, or a claim that needs a court or attorney process. The title company or attorney will say so, and that honesty is valuable: it tells the seller which professional to bring in while there is still time to act, instead of letting the issue surface at closing.

Organizing the file so professionals can use it

One folder, in a clear order, beats several scattered piles. Keep originals together in a safe place and work from copies. Divide the file by the sections of this guide — ownership, loans, taxes and insurance, condition and work, occupancy and services — and add a cover sheet listing each document, its date, and any item still missing. Note the date on every new document and keep a short log of calls and requests, including who you spoke with and what was said.

Label estimates and unknowns honestly, and keep personal account details out of copies shared broadly: share the file through the professional handling the sale, and ask that professional what should be redacted or withheld from general circulation. An organized file does not just look professional; it shortens every review that follows, because no one has to ask twice for the same paper.

How a complete file helps at closing

At closing, the title company checks ownership, clears claims, holds funds and documents in escrow, and records the transfer. Every document gathered early feeds that work: the deed and authority papers confirm who may sign, the loan and lien papers produce accurate payoff figures, the tax and association records complete the settlement statement, and the condition and occupancy papers answer the buyer's and inspector's questions before they become objections. A seller who arrives at closing with the file already reviewed will spend the appointment confirming figures, not discovering them.

After closing, keep copies of the settlement statement, the recorded deed, and every payoff and release document with the property file. The seller's file becomes the record of a completed transfer — and the reference point if any question about the sale arises later.

Documents to have ready

  • The deed and any prior title policy from the original purchase.
  • Will, trust, probate, or entity documents showing who may act.
  • Statements and written payoff figures for every mortgage and home equity line.
  • Notices and releases for any liens, judgments, or claims.
  • Property tax bills and payment receipts.
  • Insurance policies and association statements, if any.
  • Permits, contractor receipts, warranties, and a dated list of work performed.
  • Leases, occupancy agreements, and service contracts, if any.
  • Contact information for every owner, lender, and association.
  • A cover sheet listing what is present, what is missing, and what has been requested.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a paid-off loan was formally released in the records.
  • Using a monthly statement balance as the payoff amount.
  • Leaving a loan, lien, or owner off the list.
  • Assuming one owner can sign on behalf of everyone.
  • Describing an unknown condition as a known fact.
  • Waiting until closing week to gather the file.
  • Sharing originals or sensitive account details more broadly than needed.

Questions to ask before the next step

  • Who owns this property, and who has authority to sell it?
  • Which loans, liens, and claims are recorded against it today?
  • What are the exact written payoff figures, and through what dates are they valid?
  • Are taxes, insurance, and association dues current?
  • What work has been done, and which of it was permitted and approved?
  • Does anyone occupy the property, and under what written agreement?
  • Which documents are missing, and where has each one been requested?
  • Which questions belong with an attorney or a tax professional?
